How to Delegate a Task and Actually Follow Up

Updated October 19, 2026 · 5 min read

Most people who say delegating does not work have only done half of it. They hand the task off and forget it, and two weeks later it is still not done. Delegating works when the follow-up is part of the job.

What to delegate

Ask: Does this need my skill, my decision or my presence, or just a person? If someone else could do it about 80% as well, it is a good candidate. Booking travel, data entry and routine replies are typical.

Keep the tasks that need your judgment, and the ones with serious consequences where you are the only person who knows enough to do it right.

The five parts of a good handoff

  1. What done looks like. One sentence. "Flights booked, confirmation emailed to me."
  2. The deadline. A real date, not "soon".
  3. What they can decide alone. Spending limit, choices, who to contact.
  4. When to ask you. Name the situations that need your input.
  5. When you will check. Say it out loud. "I'll look on Thursday morning."

The follow-up list

This is the step most people skip. Keep one short list with three columns: the task, who has it, and the date you will check. Look at it once a day or once a week. When the date comes, ask. A friendly question is enough.

Common mistakes

  • "It's faster if I do it myself." True today, expensive every week after that.
  • Delegating with no date. There is nothing to follow up on.
  • Taking the task back at the first mistake. Give feedback, and let them finish.

Frequently asked questions

Why do delegated tasks fall through the cracks?

Because nobody owns the follow-up. Handing a task off is only half the job; you also need a note of who has it and a date to check.

What should I delegate first?

Tasks that need a person but not your specific skill, decision or presence, such as booking, data entry and routine replies.
